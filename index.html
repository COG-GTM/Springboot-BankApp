00 Executive summary

The carrier's binding constraint is no longer capital. It is engineering velocity.

Every strategic ambition a property & casualty insurer holds — entering a new line, repricing risk faster than the market, meeting a regulator's deadline, defending against a breach it also underwrites — now runs through the same chokepoint: the speed and safety with which its engineering organization can change software. And most of that organization is not building the future. It is maintaining the past.

Industry studies put as much as 84% of developer time into maintenance and toil rather than new capability,1 and the majority of carriers still run their policy, billing, and claims on platforms architected decades ago.2 Layer on three regulatory regimes arriving at once — IFRS 17, DORA, and the EU AI Act — and a post-Mythos threat landscape where the window from disclosure to exploit is measured in hours, and the modernization backlog stops being an IT line item. It becomes the limit on the business.

Neither domain expertise alone nor a coding copilot alone closes that gap. Modernization stalls because there are not enough skilled hands to do the repetitive, high-volume engineering work — and copilots make those hands type faster without removing the work itself. The unlock is to combine deep insurance delivery capability with engineering throughput that scales independently of headcount.

That is the premise of this paper. Infosys brings the domain depth, Guidewire muscle, and regulated-delivery governance — now deepened by its acquisition of Stratus, a specialist P&C transformation firm with 450+ professionals and Guidewire at the core of its practice.3 Cognition brings Devin, an autonomous software engineer that plans, writes, tests, ships, and documents whole units of work in parallel — and bills on consumption, not seats. Together they convert the modernization backlog from a capacity problem into a throughput advantage.

01 The challenge

Tech debt has become a strategic liability, not a maintenance backlog

For most carriers, the core was built for a slower world. Policy administration, rating, billing, and claims systems were customized heavily over twenty or thirty years, then left to accumulate undocumented logic, brittle integrations, and framework versions several generations behind. The people who understood the original design have retired. What remains is an estate that is expensive to change, risky to touch, and increasingly impossible to staff.

This matters now for four reasons that compound on each other:

The skills cliff is real and widening.

The senior engineers who can safely modernize a legacy policy admin system or a deeply customized Guidewire implementation are scarce and expensive — and the talent market is moving toward AI-native delivery, not COBOL or legacy Java maintenance. Carriers cannot hire their way out of the backlog at any reasonable cost, and traditional staff augmentation simply rents the bottleneck rather than removing it.

Three regulations are arriving in the same window.

IFRS 17 reshaped insurance financial reporting and left carriers with sprawling remediation around actuarial and finance systems. DORA is in force, demanding demonstrable operational resilience and an auditable record of every material change to critical systems. And the EU AI Act classifies pricing and underwriting models as high-risk, with obligations — documentation, traceability, model cards, human oversight — phasing in through 2026.5 Each one lands on the same overloaded engineering org, and each one demands evidence that today is produced by hand, after the fact.

Security moves at machine speed — and carriers underwrite the risk.

In the post-Mythos era, the interval between a vulnerability being disclosed and being exploited at scale has collapsed to hours. A legacy estate with hundreds of services running outdated frameworks and unpatched dependencies is exposed precisely where it is slowest to change. For an insurer the exposure is doubled: a carrier that writes cyber cover has to defend its own posture to the same standard it prices for its customers.

Modernization programs run on a timeline the business can no longer accept.

A core migration or a major Guidewire upgrade is typically scoped in years. The delay is rarely about architecture or design — those decisions are made early. It is about the sheer volume of repetitive engineering in between: converting configuration, rewriting integrations, mapping data, regenerating tests, and regression-checking every release. That volume is exactly the work that does not need a scarce senior architect — and exactly the work that has, until now, consumed them.

When the backlog of repetitive engineering work sets the pace of the business, the answer is not more hands on the same work. It is to take the work off the critical path entirely.

02 Why a different delivery model

Domain depth and autonomous engineering, working as one engine

The market has offered carriers two partial answers, and neither closes the gap on its own.

System integrators bring the domain knowledge, the platform certifications, and the program governance regulated delivery demands — but their economics are tied to people, so a backlog measured in millions of engineering hours stays a function of how many people can be hired and retained. Coding assistants help individual developers write lines faster, but they do not own a unit of work end to end: they do not run the tests, open the pull requests, remediate across a fleet of repositories, or leave an audit trail. They accelerate typing; they do not remove the workload.

Modernization needs both halves joined: the judgment, accountability, and insurance context that only a domain-deep partner provides, combined with engineering throughput that scales with the size of the problem rather than the size of the team. That is the model this partnership puts in market.

03 Where it lands first

Four use cases where the joint model changes the economics

These are the workloads where toil dominates the cost curve and the outcome is measurable — the natural entry points for a joint Infosys × Cognition engagement. Each pairs Infosys-led domain judgment with Devin-driven execution.

01
Core modernization · Guidewire

Guidewire upgrades and cloud migration, compressed from years to quarters

The problem

Carriers run heavily customized Guidewire PolicyCenter, BillingCenter, and ClaimCenter on-prem, often several versions behind. The path to Guidewire Cloud is gated not by design decisions but by the volume of configuration conversion, integration rework, data mapping, and regression testing in between.

The joint approach

Infosys and Stratus lead the program — target architecture, configuration design, and governance, grounded in deep Guidewire delivery experience. Devin absorbs the repetitive build: rewriting integration adapters, converting configuration, generating and maintaining test suites, and running regression in parallel across modules.

The outcome

The engineering-heavy phases of a migration collapse from quarters to weeks. Scarce Guidewire architects are freed for design and exception-handling rather than grunt work, and every change carries its own audit trail into the cloud target.

Illustrative outcome shape"Integration and regression workstreams that scoped at six months delivered in six weeks — the architects spent their time on design decisions, not test scaffolding."
02
Tech-debt & security · fleet scale

Fleet-wide framework, dependency, and CVE remediation at machine speed

The problem

Beyond the core, carriers run a sprawl of policy admin, rating, document, and integration services on Java and .NET stacks that are years behind on framework versions, carry open CVEs, and lack reliable tests. Post-Mythos, every unpatched service is an exposure the carrier also has to defend as an underwriter of cyber risk.

The joint approach

Infosys owns estate triage, prioritization, and change governance. Devin runs as a fleet — parallel agents across hundreds of repositories — upgrading frameworks, patching dependencies, remediating CVEs, and regenerating the tests that prove nothing broke, all under human review.

The outcome

Hundreds of services brought current in days and weeks rather than quarters. Remediation becomes a continuous capability the carrier can sustain, so the estate stays current and the security posture is demonstrable to regulators and reinsurers alike.

Illustrative outcome shape"Two hundred services patched to a supported framework baseline in days, with regression tests generated alongside — matching the speed of the threat for the first time."
03
Data modernization · AI readiness

Building the AI-ready data foundation across the insurance value chain

The problem

Underwriting, claims, and finance data sit fragmented across legacy stores with inconsistent lineage and quality. AI ambitions — smarter underwriting, faster claims triage, better pricing — stall on the unglamorous data engineering required to make that data trustworthy and accessible.

The joint approach

Infosys and Stratus define the cloud data architecture and governance — drawing on established data-modernization practice across underwriting, claims, and finance. Devin builds the volume: pipelines, transformations, data-quality checks, and the migration of legacy ETL and reporting into the modern target.

The outcome

A governed, AI-ready data foundation delivered in a fraction of the usual time — turning fragmented legacy data into a platform for analytics and decisioning across the value chain, with the engineering done once and done with lineage intact.

Illustrative outcome shape"The data foundation that was the year-one blocker for the carrier's AI roadmap became a one-quarter workstream — the models had clean, governed data to build on."
04
Compliance · evidence by default

IFRS 17, DORA, and EU AI Act evidence as a byproduct of delivery

The problem

Three regimes — IFRS 17 financial reporting, DORA operational resilience, and the EU AI Act's high-risk obligations for pricing and underwriting — each demand documentation, traceability, change records, and model cards. Today that evidence is assembled by hand, after the code ships, as a separate and costly exercise.

The joint approach

Infosys brings the regulatory and control framework — what evidence each regime requires and how it must be governed. Devin makes that evidence a byproduct of the same workflow that ships the code: every change produces a replayable trace, change records and model cards emerge automatically rather than being reconstructed.

The outcome

Delivery is audit-ready by default. The cost of compliance folds into the cost of delivery instead of stacking on top of it, and the carrier can answer a regulator or an examiner from the system of record rather than a manual paper trail.

Illustrative outcome shape"The DORA evidence pack and the AI Act model cards were generated from the delivery workflow itself — compliance stopped being a project of its own."
04 The joint delivery model

One team, two engines — humans set intent, agents do the volume

The partnership does not bolt a tool onto a staffing model. It changes how the work is organized. Domain-led pods from Infosys and Stratus own the outcome, the architecture, and the regulator-facing accountability. They direct fleets of Devin agents that carry out the high-volume engineering, with humans reviewing and approving at the decision points that matter.

Direct

Infosys-led pods own the program

Insurance and Guidewire experts set the target architecture, scope the work into agent-ready units, define the controls, and stay accountable for the outcome and the regulator relationship.

Execute

Devin runs the work in parallel

Govern

Humans review at the decision points; every change leaves a replayable trace. Compliance evidence and security posture are produced by the workflow, not reconstructed after it.
